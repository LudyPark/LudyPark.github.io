Filtering battery state under driving load

What a rider pays to swap a battery depends on how full the old one is. This battery could not report that number itself, so the service estimated it from voltage. While riding, voltage drops for reasons that have nothing to do with remaining charge. A swap during one of those dips could be priced at almost nothing.

Scope: production battery-charge estimator · My role: filter design and post-release defect fixes

1. Voltage dips while riding; the charge does not

The tracker measured terminal voltage under load, not open-circuit voltage: Vterminal = OCV − I × R. During driving, current through internal resistance made the observed voltage fall even when stored charge had not changed. Field data showed temporary drops of 0.3–1.9 V.

The original conversion mapped 76–80 V linearly to a 0–100% state of charge (SOC). A 1 V sag therefore appeared as a roughly 25 percentage-point SOC drop. If a swap happened in that window, the price calculation could approach zero.

2. Two wrong assumptions, two rejected shortcuts

Two assumptions in the input stream were wrong. GPS/LBS packets could repeat the previous heartbeat voltage from a cache, which distorted the effective time constant of the filter; only heartbeat packets now enter this calculation. And speed 0 was not a stop signal: about half of driving heartbeats reported zero during GPS gaps. The collector's no-moving-GPS-fix signal determines a confirmed stop; speed is used only by the absolute-drop guard.

I rejected a fixed threshold because the server received voltage but not enough information to distinguish load sag from a genuine low-SOC change. Increasing transmission frequency also did not solve the problem. At a 30-second interval, a five-minute drive supplies about ten sag samples; with α = 0.15, 0.85¹⁰ ≈ 0.2, so about 80% of the sag would still enter the estimate. It would make recovery faster while bringing the driving drop back. Coulomb counting was unavailable because packets had no current measurement; a Kalman filter required a state model and covariance work disproportionate to this path.

3. Decision: a causal three-stage filter

The live path uses only past samples. I kept input validation, outlier detection, and smoothing separate so a bad value could be explained by the right step rather than by one opaque threshold.

StagePurposeProduction values
ClampReject values outside the valid voltage range.76–80 V
Causal HampelJudge an input against recent history without future samples.3-sample window; median ± 2.0σ; σ = 1.4826 × MAD; MIN_SIGMA = 0.5 V
Absolute-drop guardRetain a second safety check, relaxed at low SOC.1.5 V; 2.0 V below 20%; 3.0 V below 10%; only when speed > 5
Asymmetric EMAUse different response rates for driving and a confirmed stopped recovery.driving α = 0.15; stopped-and-rising α = 0.6
StateStore recent history outside the pod.Redis: 9 samples, 72-hour TTL

The median/MAD pair was intentional. A mean and standard deviation move toward the outlier being tested; the median and MAD stay useful when a sudden sag enters the window. The sigma floor produces an effective 1.0 V minimum Hampel threshold: normal 0.3–0.5 V packet-to-packet discharge can pass, while a 2–3 V load sag is still rejected. Fast smoothing applies only when the vehicle is confirmed stopped and the value is rising. A stopped value that falls still uses the slower rate because a GPS gap can make a moving vehicle look stopped.

4. What changed after release

Three production cases changed the filter after release.

  1. MAD could become zero. Parked voltages were nearly identical, so MAD and sigma approached zero and ordinary variation became an outlier. MIN_SIGMA = 0.5 V fixed that failure mode.
  2. A causal window could reject a real recovery. When its recent history contained only sag values, the legitimate voltage rebound immediately after stopping was flagged and SOC remained stuck. A confirmed stopped, upward movement is physically consistent with terminal voltage returning toward OCV, so that direction now bypasses the rejection.
  3. A real low-SOC manual swap looked like an outlier. Three consecutive stopped-state outliers reset the history. This handles a battery replacement that happened without the app flow.

A charging spike of 82.5 V also made the clamp necessary: without it, one impossible reading could seed a history that held the estimate near 100%.

5. The price is fixed at a stop

Display and pricing do not use the same value blindly. The price uses the latest confirmed stopped-state voltage, is valid for ten minutes, and is invalidated if driving distance increases. The dashboard is based on unfiltered raw voltage, so it can disagree during a drive. With one voltage sensor, matching that dashboard and suppressing load-induced drops cannot both be guaranteed; operations chose stable in-drive behavior.

If Redis history is unavailable, the outlier check is skipped but smoothing remains because the prior SOC comes from the vehicle record. A spike can therefore enter the EMA. Entering that fallback is not yet visible in monitoring; closing that gap is the next piece of work here.

6. Verification

  • Ran recorded voltage traces through the existing behavior, stop-only handling, EMA, and median-plus-EMA, comparing maximum in-drive drop with time to converge after stopping.
  • Selected α = 0.15 from the bound that a 1 V sag should not show more than about 3.8 percentage points of display decline per heartbeat.
  • Checked that every decision uses past samples only, so the filter can stay on the live path.
  • Tested range limits and outliers separately from gradual normal discharge.

7. Result

On recorded voltage-trace simulations, the maximum displayed SOC drop in the affected driving pattern fell from about 25 percentage points to about 6 percentage points. This was a trace-based comparison, not a production A/B measurement. Voltage-only estimation still leaves about 6 points of movement under load, but the swap price is fixed only at a stop, where the estimate has converged.
